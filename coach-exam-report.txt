The context holds the actual lift, the actual weight and the actual completed session, so a coach that reads them can say what to do next week and a coach that does not can only say 'progressive overload'.

USER:  my squat's been stuck for a bit, what should I do
COACH: When a squat plateaus, it's usually one of three things: technique, recovery, or how we're pacing the sets. Since you're hitting 45kg for sets of 8, we have a solid foundation to work with, but we need to change the stimulus to break through.

Here are the three best ways to unstuck it:

1. Change the rep range: If you've been grinding at 8 reps for weeks, your body has adapted. We can swap you to a strength-focused rep range (like 3 sets of 5) with slightly more weight to build raw force, or go the other way with higher reps (sets of 10-12) to build muscle volume.
2. Pause at the bottom: Swapping to a Pause Squat for a block. Taking a 2-second pause at the very bottom of the lift destroys the "stretch reflex" (the bounce), forcing your quads and glutes to work twice as hard to get you back up.
3. Manage the fatigue: If you're feeling beaten down before you even unrack the bar, we might just need to pull a couple of sets off your other leg work for a week or two so you aren't carrying deep fatigue into your squat sessions.

Before we change anything in your plan, how have these heavy sets of 8 actually been feeling lately—is there a specific part of the lift where you feel like you're hitting a wall?

USER:  ok, and how long before I'd expect that to move
COACH: If we swap the rep range or move you to a variation like pause squats, you can expect to see the needle move within 3 to 4 weeks. 

That timeline comes down to how your body adapts. The first 1 to 2 weeks are mostly neurological—your brain and nervous system getting efficient at the new movement pattern or the heavier load. By weeks 3 and 4, you start seeing the actual strength and muscle adaptations translate back into your main lift. 

If we don't see progress by the end of a 4-week block, that's our signal that the bottleneck is likely recovery (sleep or food) rather than the training stimulus itself. 

Which of those options feels like the right match for where you're at right now—shifting the reps, trying pause squats, or backing off the extra leg volume for a bit?

## specific-why-this-exercise
   why this case exists: VISION: a coach has a reason for every choice, and the app promises 'Why this exercise' on screen. Asked in chat, the answer must be about this session's actual order and this person's actual plan, not a textbook paragraph on compound lifts.

USER:  why are the romanian deadlifts after the squats and not before
COACH: Squats are programmed before your Romanian Deadlifts because of how much energy and core stability they demand. 

Back squats are your primary compound lift today (a Tier 1 lift). They require your entire nervous system, legs, and core to be completely fresh so you can safely handle the heavy load and maintain solid form. 

Romanian Deadlifts, while still a heavy compound movement, focus heavily on your hamstrings and glutes (a hip hinge pattern). If you did RDLs first, your lower back and hamstrings would already be fatigued, which would make it much harder—and riskier—to keep your torso upright and stable when you get under the squat barbell. 

Putting the biggest, most system-taxing lift first is the safest and most effective way to build strength. How has your lower back been feeling during those RDLs lately?

## voice-how-to-short-steps
   why this case exists: Ashley, 24 Sep 2026, after reading four paragraphs on how to deadlift: a how-to is up to three short steps, with the full guide one tap away ("short steps"). The first turn should be three lines or fewer plus the offer; the second, asked for, may be longer but still short lines, not paragraphs.

USER:  how do I do a deadlift
COACH: 1. Set your feet: Stand with your feet hip-width apart, with the barbell cutting directly across the middle of your shoelaces.
2. Hinge and grip: Bend at your hips and knees to grip the bar just outside your shins, keeping your arms completely straight.
3. Pull the slack out: Flatten your back, push your chest up, and pull the bar tight against your shins until you feel your hamstrings and upper back load up, then push the floor away to stand.

[Watch Deadlift Tutorial on YouTube](www.youtube.com/results?search_query=deadlift+tutorial+form)

That setup is the secret to keeping your lower back safe. Are you planning on hitting these in the gym today, or just getting a feel for the movement?

USER:  Full form guide
COACH: 1. The Setup
* Foot Position: Stand with your feet hip-width apart. When you look down, the barbell should cut directly across the middle of your feet (about an inch away from your shins).
* The Grip: Hinge at your hips and bend your knees just enough to grip the bar. Your hands should be placed just outside your shins. Keep your arms completely straight—they act as ropes, not levers.

2. Bracing and Tension
* Shin Contact: Drop your hips slightly until your shins touch the bar. Do not let the bar roll forward; it must stay over your mid-foot.
* Pull the Slack Out: Pull up on the bar just enough to hear it click against the weights. This creates full-body tension. 
* Set your Spine: Flatten your back completely. Squeeze your armpits tight—imagine trying to crush oranges in your armpits to engage your lats.

3. The Pull
* Push the Floor Away: Do not try to "pull" the bar up with your arms or back. Instead, initiate the lift by driving your feet hard into the floor, as if you are trying to push the earth away from you.
* Bar Path: Keep the bar in contact with your legs the entire way up. If it drifts forward, it places unnecessary stress on your lower back.
* The Lockout: Stand tall by squeezing your glutes at the top. Do not hyperextend or lean backward at the lockout.

4. The Descent
* Hinge First: To lower the bar, push your hips backward as if reaching for a wall behind you. 
* Clear the Knees: Keep the bar close to your thighs. Once the bar passes your knees, bend your knees to place the plates quietly back on the floor. Reset your setup completely before the next rep.

[Watch Deadlift Tutorial on YouTube](www.youtube.com/results?search_query=deadlift+tutorial+form)

That's the complete checklist to build a solid pull. Do you want to focus on any specific part of this, like how to breathe and brace, or are you ready to put it into practice?
